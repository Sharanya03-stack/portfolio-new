The Meme Equation: Simulating Virality in the Age of Algorithms

Authors: Sharanya N, Arya Sham Pandit, Khan Saif S
sce24ic034@sairamtap.edu.in, sce24ic041@sairamtap.edu.in, sce24am054@sairamtap.edu.in
Department of Science and Humanities, Sri Sairam College of Engineering, Bangalore

Abstract

In today's hyperconnected world, memes spread rapidly across digital communities. This study applies a mathematical model traditionally used to track infectious disease outbreaks—the SIR (Susceptible–Infected–Recovered) model—to analyze how internet memes propagate. We represent social media users as nodes in a network where encountering and sharing a meme parallels infection dynamics: users are exposed, actively share the content, and eventually lose interest. By varying the transmission rate (β) and the attention decay rate (γ), we simulate how memes rise, reach peak virality, and subside across algorithmic feeds.

Keywords: Meme Virality, SIR Model, Social Networks, Information Diffusion, Mathematical Modeling

1. Introduction

Several studies have examined information diffusion in social networks. Early works used epidemiological models to analyze the spread of rumors, news, and cultural artifacts. More recent research incorporates agent-based models, network topology, and game theory to characterize online information flow. Applying compartmental differential equations specifically to short-lifecycle internet memes provides a clear framework for studying attention saturation and algorithmic amplification.

2. Literature Review

Early foundational work leveraged epidemiological models to simulate rumor and information spreading. Daley and Kendall (1964) compared rumor propagation with epidemic dynamics, while Leskovec et al. (2007) analyzed cascading behavior in viral marketing. Weng et al. (2013) examined virality prediction and community structure on microblogging platforms.

Unlike general news diffusion, meme virality depends heavily on rapid relatability, format remixing, and steep attention decay, making compartmental modeling with explicit transmission and recovery parameters particularly instructive.

3. Methodology

We apply the SIR (Susceptible–Infected–Recovered) compartmental model to represent meme diffusion across a population of size N:

  • Susceptible (S): Users who have not yet encountered the meme.
  • Infected (I): Users who have seen and are actively sharing or engaging with the meme.
  • Recovered (R): Users who have seen the meme, lost interest, and stopped sharing.

Model variables and parameters:

  • S(t), I(t), R(t) denote the number of users in each compartment at time t.
  • N denotes the total network population (N = S + I + R).
  • β denotes the effective sharing / transmission rate.
  • γ denotes the attention decay / recovery rate.
dS/dt = − β · (S · I) / N
dI/dt = β · (S · I) / N − γ · I
dR/dt = γ · I

These coupled ordinary differential equations are integrated numerically to evaluate infection peaks and lifecycle duration across different parameter regimes.

4. Simulation and Results

Consider a simulated online community of N = 10,000 users. At t = 0, I0 = 10 initial seed adopters share the meme, leaving S0 = 9,990 susceptible users and R0 = 0 recovered users. When β = 0.3 and γ = 0.1 (basic reproduction ratio R0 = β/γ = 3.0), active sharing rises to a clear viral peak before attention saturation transitions the population into recovery.

5. Discussion: Factors Driving Virality

Our simulation confirms that virality depends on the ratio between transmission rate (β) and attention decay (γ). In real-world social platforms, additional structural factors shape these rates:

  • Algorithmic Feed Amplification: Recommendation engines (short-form video feeds and ranked timelines) dynamically increase effective β for high-engagement content clusters.
  • High-Degree Network Hubs: Creators and community hubs expose large susceptible sub-networks simultaneously, compressing time-to-peak.
  • Contextual & Temporal Relevance: Topical alignment with cultural events temporarily elevates sharing propensity.

6. Extensions for Future Work

  • SIRS Cyclic Re-Adoption: Allowing recovered nodes to return to the susceptible pool to model recurring meme templates.
  • Sentiment-Weighted Transmission: Conditioning β on emotional valence and format adaptability.
  • Time-Varying Decay γ(t): Modeling accelerating fatigue as platform saturation increases.

7. Conclusion

Epidemiological compartmental modeling offers a clear quantitative foundation for understanding information diffusion and meme lifecycles in algorithmic networks. Evaluating the interplay between sharing velocity (β) and attention decay (γ) helps explain why certain digital artifacts achieve widespread adoption while others dissipate rapidly.
